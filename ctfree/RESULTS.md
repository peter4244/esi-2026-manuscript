## Study population

The analytic cohort consisted of 9,240 COPDGene subjects (Supplemental Table S1). It comprised 4,037 smokers without airflow obstruction (GOLD 0), 1,119 subjects with PRISm, and 4,084 subjects with GOLD 1 to 4 airflow obstruction. Mean baseline ESI increased monotonically with severity, from 0.90 in GOLD 0 to 8.30 in GOLD 4 subjects. ESI changed little with bronchodilation: the mean change from pre- to post-bronchodilator ESI was −0.09 (SD 0.82) among the 9,234 subjects with both measurements.

The COPD-related outcomes studied were all-cause mortality, respiratory mortality, respiratory exacerbations, and lung function decline. The median follow-up periods were 10.8 years for all-cause and respiratory mortality, 10.4 years for respiratory exacerbations, and 9.7 years for lung function decline among the 5,414 subjects with at least one follow-up spirometry visit.

## Selection of diagnostic thresholds for the CT-free classifications

Both CT-free classifications required a threshold for the number of minor criteria required for the COPD minor pathway, and the ESI classification additionally required an ESI threshold. We explored multiple measures for identifying optimal threshold values, evaluating their performance using five-fold cross validation. Our final thresholds were based on the macro-F1 measure, which had the desirable effect of giving equal importance to correct assignment for each of the four groups despite the large difference in sample size between groups (Supplemental Table S2). The selected rules assigned COPD minor at ≥ 2 minor criteria for both the ESI and NoCT classifications, and the optimal ESI threshold for recovery of MD-COPD subgroups was ≥ 1.50.

## Agreement between MD-COPD and CT-free classifications

The cross-classification between MD-COPD and the two CT-free classifications is shown in Figure 1. The ESI classification correctly classified 89.0% of subjects compared to 85.0% for the NoCT classification (p<0.005). At the level of the diagnostic pathways, for the no COPD and COPD major pathway groups, both classifications had excellent agreement with MD-COPD (>97% accuracy for both), but agreement was weaker for the COPD minor and airflow limitation only (AFL-only) pathways (Supplemental Table S3). For COPD minor, the ESI-based and NoCT classifications had 59% and 60% accuracy, respectively. For AFL-only, the ESI classification outperformed the NoCT classification (36% versus 25% accuracy, p<0.005), but both groups assigned substantially more subjects to AFL-only than MD-COPD did. For the NoCT classification, it was not possible to properly assign subjects with obstruction and positive CT minor criteria to the AFL-only pathway since the CT criteria were unavailable with no adequate surrogate included in the classification.

## Clinical outcomes by diagnostic group

The benefit of the MD-COPD classification over the fixed ratio results from removing the COPD diagnosis from low-risk individuals with airflow obstruction via the AFL-only pathway and by applying the diagnosis to a high-risk subset of non-obstructed smokers via the COPD minor pathway. To determine whether the ESI and NoCT classifications retained this property, we compared the risks of mortality and exacerbations for the AFL-only and COPD minor pathway groups across the three multidimensional classifications. To ensure comparable assessment, we defined a noCOPD group (n=3,856) that consisted of subjects assigned as noCOPD by all three methods.

Crude risks for the AFL-only pathway are shown in Figure 2, with crude and adjusted risks in Supplemental Figure S1 and Supplemental Tables S4 and S5. For the ESI classification, its AFL-only group had similar risks to those of the MD-COPD AFL-only group for all-cause mortality (crude rate ratio 1.25 versus 1.10, p=0.25) and respiratory exacerbations (1.11 versus 1.15, p=0.73). Respiratory deaths in these groups were too few to estimate risk. For the NoCT classification, its AFL-only group had significantly higher risks than the MD-COPD AFL-only group for all-cause mortality (crude rate ratio 1.52 versus 1.10, p=0.002) and respiratory exacerbations (1.64 versus 1.15, p<0.002). Its crude rate ratio for respiratory mortality was 8.07, against 1.80 for MD-COPD, which could not be compared statistically because the MD-COPD AFL-only group had only 2 respiratory deaths.

Crude risks for the COPD minor and major pathways are also shown in Figure 2. For the COPD minor pathway group, the three multidimensional classifications had similar risk profiles, with the NoCT and ESI estimates numerically lower than MD-COPD on all three outcomes. The differences were significant for all-cause mortality and exacerbations; respiratory deaths were too few to compare (Supplemental Table S4). For COPD major, risk was the highest for the NoCT classification on all three outcomes. Compared directly with MD-COPD, the relative risks of the COPD major group were 18% to 28% higher under the NoCT classification and 5% to 9% higher under the ESI classification (all p<0.005, Supplemental Table S4). This is consistent with the reclassification of COPD major subjects into the AFL-only pathway. The NoCT classification moved 833 such subjects, raising the risk of its AFL-only group and leaving a smaller, higher risk COPD major group (2,976 versus 3,809 subjects), whereas the ESI classification moved 350 subjects whose risk did not differ significantly from that of subjects both classifications assign to AFL-only (Figure 3).

Finally, we estimated annualized change in FEV₁ for every group against the same common reference, adjusting for multiple covariates including baseline post-bronchodilator FEV₁ (Supplemental Table S6). While all three classifications had significant FEV₁ decline in COPD major pathway subjects, only MD-COPD also had significant decline in the COPD minor pathway, with an AFL-only group with no significant decline. Conversely, both CT-free classifications had significant decline in the AFL-only group, indicating a degree of contamination in that group (−4.9 mL/yr and −4.3 mL/yr, p=0.68). The CT-free classifications also failed to produce a COPD minor group with significant FEV₁ decline.

## Emphysema and Airway Wall Thickness by MD-COPD and ESI classifications

Since the NoCT classification misdiagnosed high risk individuals as not having COPD, we discarded this classification and focused on the cross-tabulation of the ESI classification compared to MD-COPD. To better understand the clinical characteristics and risk profile of these classifications, we examined the levels of CT emphysema and airway wall thickening in both classifications stratified by the presence of airflow obstruction (Figure 4).

In subjects with preserved spirometry (n = 5,156), COPD diagnoses occurred only via the COPD minor pathway. In the cross-tabulation, 74.8% of preserved spirometry subjects were classified as noCOPD by both classifications, 13.8% as COPD by both, 9.7% as COPD by ESI alone and 1.6% as COPD by CT alone. As expected, when comparing the CT-only-COPD to the ESI-only-COPD subjects, the CT-only subjects had a higher prevalence of visually assessed emphysema (100% versus 1.2%, p<0.001) and quantitative emphysema (mean %LAA-950 1.91 versus 1.24, p=0.001). Visual airway wall thickness was also present in higher proportions in CT-only versus ESI-only subjects (100% versus 0.6%, p<0.001). Surprisingly, ESI levels were not significantly different between groups (mean 0.90 versus 0.97, p=0.13), which indicates that in subjects with preserved spirometry, it was a higher prevalence of respiratory symptoms that drove diagnosis via the COPD minor pathway (mMRC dyspnea score of 2 or greater in 84.4% versus 36.5% and SGRQ total score of 25 or greater in 94.0% versus 58.8% of ESI-only versus CT-only subjects, both p<0.001; Figure 4).

In subjects with airflow obstruction (n = 4,084), COPD diagnoses occurred only via the COPD major pathway in which only one minor criterion was necessary for diagnosis. This means that, by definition, ESI-only-COPD subjects did not have any visual emphysema or airway wall thickness. In the cross-tabulation, 4.7% of subjects were classified as AFL-only by both classifications, 84.7% as COPD major by both, 2.0% as COPD by ESI alone, and 8.6% as COPD by CT alone. In CT-only-COPD subjects, 77.7% had visually assessed emphysema and 47.4% had visually assessed airway wall thickening. Interestingly, there was a disconnect between visual emphysema and quantitative CT measures in these subsets, because levels of CT quantitative emphysema were nominally higher in the ESI-only group, though the difference between ESI-only and CT-only was not significant (mean %LAA-950 4.80 versus 4.09, p=0.53). In the obstructed subset, ESI-only-COPD subjects had higher ESI values (mean 2.25 versus 1.21 in CT-only-COPD, p<0.001), indicating that ESI was a major contributor to the COPD classifications in these subjects.

We also evaluated outcome risks for all-cause mortality and exacerbations by cross-tabulated diagnostic category within obstructed and non-obstructed subjects (Figure 3, Supplemental Table S7). There were not enough respiratory death outcomes for statistical testing in these subgroups. In the preserved spirometry subset, all three COPD groups had significantly elevated risk; exacerbation risk was highest in subjects diagnosed with COPD by both classifications, and all-cause mortality risk was highest in the CT-only group. In the obstructed subjects, elevated risk was observed only in subjects diagnosed with COPD by both classifications.

## Relationship of ESI to the visual CT criteria

We examined how closely ESI tracks the visual CT emphysema and airway wall thickness criteria for which it is a surrogate. Mean ESI increased monotonically across both scales, from 1.04 to 6.62 across the six levels of visual emphysema and from 0.96 to 3.32 across the three levels of airway wall thickening (Supplemental Table S8). Among subjects with airflow limitation, ESI discriminated both visual emphysema and definite wall thickening with an area under the curve of 0.75, whereas among those with preserved spirometry the values fell to 0.56 and 0.61 (Table 2). This also corresponds to the impact of the ESI criterion on the multidimensional classification where its benefit was primarily concentrated in improving the classification of subjects with airflow limitation, whereas it was less impactful in the subset with preserved spirometry.
