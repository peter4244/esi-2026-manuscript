## Study population and classification

The analytic cohort included 9,240 participants: 1,119 with preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm), 4,037 with preserved spirometry (formerly referred to as Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) grade 0), 734 with GOLD 1, 1,772 with GOLD 2, 1,050 with GOLD 3, and 528 with GOLD 4. The distribution of participants across diagnostic pathway categories under the four classifications is shown in Table 1. Mean ESI increased across GOLD strata, from 0.90 in GOLD 0 to 8.30 in GOLD 4 (Table S1). ESI changed little with bronchodilation, although the change was statistically significant (mean change −0.09 [95% CI −0.11 to −0.08]).

For the modified MD-COPD classifications (NoCT and ESI classifications), the selected thresholds were ≥2 minor criteria for COPD-minor (out of 3 for NoCT and 4 for the ESI classification) and ESI ≥1.50 for the ESI criterion, corresponding to the thresholds with the highest macro-averaged F1 values (Table S2).

## Relationship of ESI to the visual CT criteria

Mean ESI increased progressively with visually assessed emphysema severity, from 1.04 with no emphysema to 6.62 with advanced destructive emphysema, and across categories of visual airway wall thickening, from 0.96 when absent to 3.32 when definite. Using the MD-COPD criteria (emphysema of at least mild severity and definite airway wall thickening), ESI discriminated visual emphysema and wall thickening with AUCs of 0.78 and 0.80, respectively, in the overall cohort and 0.75 for both criteria among participants with airflow limitation. Discrimination was lower in the subset of participants with preserved spirometry (AUC 0.56 and 0.61, respectively) (Figure 1).

## Agreement between MD-COPD and CT-free classifications

Cross-classification with MD-COPD is shown in Figure 2 and Table S3. Overall agreement was 89.0% (8,222 of 9,240) for ESI and 85.0% (7,852 of 9,240) for NoCT (P < 0.005). With MD-COPD as the reference, sensitivity and positive predictive value within the preserved spirometry groups were very similar between the NoCT and ESI classifications. For noCOPD, sensitivity was 89.3% with NoCT and 88.5% with ESI and positive predictive value 97.7% and 97.8%, respectively. For COPD-minor, sensitivity was 88.7% and 89.4% and positive predictive value 60.4% and 58.8%. The differences between the NoCT and ESI classifications were more notable in the airflow limitation groups. For AFL-only, NoCT had a sensitivity of 100% against 70.2% with ESI, but a positive predictive value of only 24.8% against 35.5% with ESI (P < 0.005). For COPD-major, the sensitivity of NoCT was 78.1%, against 90.8% with ESI, while positive predictive value was 100% with NoCT and 97.7% with ESI. Both of these results reflect that, in the absence of available CT criteria, the NoCT classification could not identify participants whose only minor criteria were related to structural lung disease and accordingly moved 833 MD-COPD COPD-major participants (21.9%) into AFL-only. With the benefit of the ESI threshold, the ESI classification was less susceptible to this misclassification (n = 350, 9.2% of all MD-COPD COPD-major participants).

## Clinical outcomes

Median follow-up was 10.8 years for mortality, 10.4 years for exacerbations, and 9.7 years for lung function decline.

The differences in outcome risk among classifications were most apparent in the AFL-only group, which under MD-COPD had unadjusted risks similar to those of the noCOPD reference (Figure 3). Compared to MD-COPD AFL-only participants, NoCT-defined AFL-only had higher unadjusted all-cause mortality and exacerbation rates (rate ratios 1.38 [1.10–1.77] and 1.42 [1.15–1.79]; both P < 0.005). ESI-defined AFL-only did not have significantly higher risk for these outcomes (rate ratio 1.14 for mortality and 0.96 for exacerbations; P > 0.2 for both, Table S4). After adjustment for covariates, NoCT-defined AFL-only still had higher risk for exacerbations (ratio of IRRs 1.33 [1.07–1.70]; P = 0.008) but not all-cause mortality (ratio of HRs 1.22; P = 0.098). ESI-defined AFL-only again did not differ from MD-COPD for either adjusted outcome (ratios 1.03 and 0.92; P > 0.2 for both) (Table S5; Figure S1). Respiratory deaths were too few for statistical comparison, though NoCT-defined AFL-only had a numerically higher rate of respiratory deaths (0.30 per 100 person-years versus 0.07 for both MD-COPD and ESI-defined AFL-only). Removing the CT criteria therefore produced an AFL-only group with higher exacerbation rates and, in unadjusted comparisons, higher mortality than that of MD-COPD, whereas substituting ESI produced an AFL-only group whose risk did not differ from that of MD-COPD.

In COPD-minor, all-cause mortality and exacerbation rates were lower under NoCT (rate ratios 0.87 [0.79–0.95]; P = 0.006 and 0.90 [0.83–0.99]; P = 0.036) and ESI (0.88 [0.80–0.97]; P = 0.008 and 0.89 [0.81–0.98]; P = 0.016) than under MD-COPD (Table S4), but not after adjustment (ratios of HRs and IRRs 0.92 to 0.95; P = 0.096 to 0.27) (Table S5). Respiratory deaths were again too few for statistical comparison.

In COPD-major, all-cause mortality and exacerbation rates were higher under NoCT (rate ratios 1.18 [1.15–1.21] and 1.22 [1.19–1.25]) and ESI (1.05 [1.04–1.07] and 1.07 [1.06–1.09]) than under MD-COPD (all P < 0.005) (Table S4), and remained higher after adjustment (ratios of HRs and IRRs 1.17 to 1.23 under NoCT and 1.07 under ESI; all P < 0.005) (Table S5). Respiratory mortality in COPD-major was also higher under NoCT (rate ratio 1.28 [1.24–1.31]) and ESI (1.09 [1.07–1.10]) than under MD-COPD, before and after adjustment (ratios of HRs 1.29 and 1.08; all P < 0.005).

Longitudinal FEV₁ decline differed across diagnostic pathways (Table S6). For all three classifications, COPD-major showed greater decline than the common noCOPD reference (−5.3, −5.1, and −5.0 mL/yr with MD-COPD, NoCT, and ESI). Significantly faster decline than the reference in COPD-minor was observed only with MD-COPD (−4.5 mL/yr; P = 0.007) and not with NoCT or ESI (−0.1 mL/yr for both). For AFL-only, the rate of decline for MD-COPD was not significant (−2.7 mL/yr; P = 0.22), but both NoCT (−4.9 mL/yr) and ESI (−4.3 mL/yr) did have significantly faster decline.

## Discordant MD-COPD and ESI classifications

We then examined participant-level concordance between the MD-COPD and ESI classifications, stratified by airflow limitation. Clinical, physiological, and CT characteristics of concordant and discordant groups are shown in Figure S2. In the preserved spirometry subset, 501 participants were ESI-only-COPD, 85 CT-only-COPD, and 714 Both-COPD. Visual emphysema and airway wall thickening were more frequent in CT-only-COPD, whereas mMRC ≥2, SGRQ ≥25, and chronic bronchitis were more frequent in ESI-only-COPD. ESI did not differ significantly between the discordant groups (P = 0.13), and only 49 of the 501 ESI-only-COPD participants (9.8%) met the ESI criterion. Thus with preserved spirometry, most of the difference between the MD-COPD and ESI classifications came from the difference in the number of minor criteria required for the COPD-minor pathway. In the subset with airflow limitation, 350 participants were CT-only-COPD, 82 ESI-only-COPD, and 3,459 Both-COPD. ESI was higher and visual emphysema less frequent in ESI-only-COPD than in CT-only-COPD, whereas %LAA-950HU did not differ significantly (Figure S2).

With preserved spirometry, all three COPD groups (ESI-only-COPD, CT-only-COPD, and Both-COPD) had higher all-cause mortality and exacerbation rates than participants classified as noCOPD by both approaches (Figure 4; Table S7). With airflow limitation, neither discordant group had significantly increased all-cause mortality or exacerbation rates compared to the participants classified as AFL-only by both methods, whereas Both-COPD had increased risks of both outcomes.
