# Discussion — CT-free MD-COPD paper

In this study, we examined whether the Emphysema Severity Index (ESI), derived from the expiratory portion of the spirometric flow-volume curve, could substitute for the CT criteria of the MD-COPD framework when CT is unavailable. We observed that using ESI in place of CT criteria preserved many of the beneficial aspects of the MD-COPD classification, especially among individuals with airflow obstruction, but it did have deficiencies in identifying higher risk individuals with preserved spirometry.

The benefits of the MD-COPD classification arise from the creation of the COPD-minor pathway, which confers the COPD label to individuals with preserved spirometry but a high symptoms burden or evidence of structural lung disease, and the AFL-only pathway which removes the COPD diagnosis from relatively low risk individuals with airflow obstruction but no clear evidence of lung structural disease. Our data show that substituting ESI for the MD-COPD CT criteria is reasonably effective at reconstituting a “low-risk” AFL-only diagnostic pathway, but less effective at isolating a “higher-risk” COPD-minor diagnostic pathway. ESI as a surrogate, while less than perfect, does perform substantially better than trying to reconstruct the MD-COPD classification without any surrogate for the CT criteria.

ESI describes the shape of the expiratory flow-volume curve, which changes with loss of elastic recoil, small airway dysfunction, and differences in lung volume and thoracic gas compression (21–23). It was a better surrogate for the CT criteria in individuals with airflow obstruction than with preserved spirometry (AUC 0.75 for both criteria, against 0.56 and 0.61), consistent with structural abnormalities that do not alter expiratory flow leaving ESI unchanged. While ESI can serve as an index for the severity of emphysema (13, 14), the relationship between ESI, emphysema (measured both visually and via measures like %LAA-950HU), and airway wall thickness is complex and cannot be simplified to a simple substitution of ESI for emphysema or airway thickness in isolation. The reality is that each of these measures captures overlapping but distinct elements of lung structural disease, and one of the advantages of the MD-COPD classification and its ESI-modified surrogate is that these classifications are grounded in the relationship between these measures and the risk of COPD-related prospective health outcomes.

Because ESI is a more effective surrogate for CT criteria in individuals with airflow obstruction, its advantages over the NoCT classification derive primarily from the characteristics of the AFL-only diagnostic pathway under the ESI classification. Without CT criteria available, the 833 MD-COPD COPD-major participants whose only minor criteria were CT findings were all reclassified as AFL-only by the NoCT classification, producing an AFL-only group that was four times the size of MD-COPD AFL-only (1,108 versus 275 participants). This transfer raised event rates in both AFL-only and COPD-major, and it eliminated the central characteristic of the AFL-only pathway, which is to remove the COPD diagnosis from obstructed individuals with relatively low risk of COPD-related outcomes. Because ESI could capture some aspects of the original CT criteria in obstructed individuals, the ESI classification misassigned many fewer COPD-major participants as AFL-only, and accordingly it preserved much of the risk differential between the AFL-only and COPD-major diagnostic pathways.

There are subsets of participants where ESI does not do very well at replacing the CT criteria. For the COPD-minor pathway, while the ESI classification did identify participants with higher risks of mortality and exacerbations relative to the noCOPD group, these unadjusted rates were 11% to 12% lower than in MD-COPD COPD-minor (P = 0.008 and 0.016), as were those under NoCT, consistent with the lower number of criteria both CT-free classifications require. The clearest case of higher-risk individuals missed by ESI is the 85 participants with preserved spirometry whom MD-COPD, but not the ESI classification, called COPD: all met both visual CT criteria, their mean ESI (0.90) was similar to that of participants classified as noCOPD by both approaches (0.89), and their unadjusted mortality rate was twice that of the reference (rate ratio 2.15 [1.34–3.28]) (Figure 4). The ESI classification also performed less well than the original MD-COPD classification for FEV₁ decline: faster decline than the reference was seen in MD-COPD COPD-minor (−4.5 mL/yr) but not in ESI-defined or NoCT-defined COPD-minor (−0.1 mL/yr for both). Even the ESI-defined AFL-only group, which otherwise performed quite well, had faster FEV₁ decline than the common reference (−4.3 mL/yr; P = 0.012), whereas MD-COPD AFL-only did not (−2.7 mL/yr; P = 0.22).

ESI has attractive characteristics as a substitute for CT criteria. It is calculated from the flow-volume curve recorded during standard spirometry, so it requires neither an additional test nor imaging, and because it uses absolute measurements it needs no ethnic or anthropometric reference equations. Unlike many other spirometric criteria, it is fairly insensitive to bronchodilation (24) which is, for the case of COPD diagnostic tools, a source of undesirable measurement variability (25). These properties are most relevant to epidemiological studies, large cohorts, and clinical settings where systematic CT assessment is not feasible.

This study has several strengths. All three classifications were applied to the same 9,240 COPDGene participants, and including the NoCT classification distinguished the effect of removing the CT criteria from that of replacing them. Long-term follow-up allowed each reclassification to be examined against mortality, exacerbations, and FEV₁ decline. Visual and quantitative CT were both available, so the visual criteria used by MD-COPD could be compared with quantitative emphysema.

Several limitations should be considered. COPDGene enrolled current and former smokers aged 45–80 years with ≥10 pack-years, and the ESI classification was developed and evaluated in the same cohort; although thresholds were cross-validated, external validation is required. MD-COPD served as the reference but is not a diagnostic gold standard, so agreement measures how well its structure is preserved rather than diagnostic accuracy, and discordance cannot establish which approach is correct. Respiratory deaths were too few in AFL-only and COPD-minor for comparison between classifications. Finally, these findings do not imply that ESI can replace chest CT when anatomical characterization is clinically required.

In conclusion, replacing the CT component of MD-COPD with ESI preserved much of the original multidimensional classification and recovered clinically relevant information lost when imaging criteria were simply removed. The recovery was concentrated in participants with airflow limitation, where ESI kept more than half of the participants whose COPD-major diagnosis rested on CT out of the AFL-only group. When chest CT is unavailable, ESI allows MD-COPD to be applied from spirometry and symptom questionnaires alone. External validation is required to establish the generalizability and clinical applicability of this framework.
