# Discussion — CT-free MD-COPD paper

In this study, we examined whether the Emphysema Severity Index (ESI), derived from the expiratory portion of the spirometric flow-volume curve, could physiologically represent part of the information contributed by chest CT to a multidimensional COPD diagnostic framework when CT is unavailable. Replacing the CT criteria with ESI preserved a high degree of agreement with the original MD-COPD classification, whereas removing CT without replacement resulted in greater redistribution across diagnostic pathways. Thus, information otherwise provided by imaging can, in part, be recovered from the spirometric examination itself.

ESI contributed most among participants with airflow limitation. Removing CT reassigned to AFL-only the MD-COPD COPD-major participants whose diagnosis rested on a CT criterion, whereas incorporating ESI kept more than half of them within COPD-major. This reclassification was reflected in clinical outcomes: the larger AFL-only group generated by CT removal had higher mortality and exacerbation rates than MD-COPD AFL-only, whereas ESI-defined AFL-only showed estimates closer to those of MD-COPD. With preserved spirometry, ESI added less: the ESI and NoCT classifications reproduced MD-COPD COPD-minor to a similar degree, and fewer than 10% of ESI-only COPD participants met the ESI criterion.

Longitudinal lung-function findings further indicate that the classifications do not simply identify more or less severe versions of the same populations. Greater FEV₁ decline was observed in COPD-major under all three classifications, whereas in AFL-only and COPD-minor the pattern under the CT-free classifications was the reverse of that under MD-COPD. These differences likely reflect redistribution across diagnostic pathways and emphasize that agreement in classification and association with individual longitudinal outcomes represent distinct properties of a multidimensional framework.

That ESI recovered part of the information contributed by CT is consistent with its relationship to structural lung abnormalities, but it does not make ESI a spirometric surrogate for CT findings in general. ESI was originally developed to quantify the physiological expression of emphysema from the expiratory portion of the flow-volume curve and evaluated against quantitative CT measures of emphysema (13, 14). In the present study, which used visual rather than quantitative CT criteria for MD-COPD, ESI increased with visual emphysema severity and was also associated with definite airway wall thickening. Among participants with airflow limitation, its AUC was 0.75 for both abnormalities (Figure 1). This association with visually assessed airway wall thickening does not imply that ESI measures airway disease itself; against quantitative CT phenotypes, its relationship has been predominantly with emphysema rather than quantitative airway abnormalities (13, 14). These observations support ESI as a physiological descriptor of structural lung disease while preserving its particular relationship to the emphysematous phenotype.

ESI discriminated the visual CT abnormalities used in MD-COPD considerably better in participants with airflow limitation (AUC, 0.75 for both criteria) than in those with preserved spirometry (0.56 and 0.61). Loss of elastic recoil, small-airway dysfunction, and differences in lung volume and thoracic gas compression can influence expiratory flow generation and the morphology of the expiratory portion of the flow-volume curve (21–23). ESI may therefore capture the integrated physiological expression of structural abnormalities even though it does not identify the anatomical lesion responsible. Conversely, CT abnormalities may be detectable without a sufficiently distinctive physiological expression. CT and ESI should therefore not be viewed as interchangeable measurements: CT depicts structural abnormalities, whereas ESI describes their expression within expiratory physiology. They may contribute to the same multidimensional classification through complementary representations of a common disease dimension. Accordingly, the contribution of ESI to this multidimensional framework should not be interpreted as diminishing the clinical importance of airway-predominant disease or of its specific characterization.

Discordance between ESI and MD-COPD further illustrated these differences. With preserved spirometry, ESI-only COPD was considerably more frequent than CT-only COPD, but both groups had higher mortality and exacerbation rates than participants classified as noCOPD by both approaches. CT-only COPD had more frequent visual emphysema and airway wall thickening, whereas ESI-only COPD more frequently met symptom criteria. Thus, without airflow limitation, CT and ESI may identify different expressions of disease rather than alternative measurements of the same abnormality.

A different pattern emerged with airflow limitation. CT-only COPD was more frequent than ESI-only COPD, and neither discordant group had significantly higher mortality or exacerbation rates than participants classified as AFL-only by both approaches, whereas participants classified as COPD by both had markedly higher risks. CT-only discordance may partly reflect visual detection of structural abnormalities without sufficient alteration of expiratory physiology to satisfy the ESI criterion. MD-COPD considers visual emphysema present at mild severity or greater, and CT-only COPD had more frequent visual emphysema than ESI-only COPD despite no significant difference in %LAA-950HU. Discordance may therefore partly reflect the distinction between visually recognizable structural abnormalities and their physiological expression rather than failure of either approach to detect disease.

The distinction between anatomical abnormality and physiological expression also places ESI within the phenotypic heterogeneity of COPD. Because ESI relates predominantly to quantitative emphysema (13, 14), among patients with similar airflow limitation it can provide information on the relative contribution of emphysematous parenchymal destruction not conveyed by FEV₁ or FEV₁/FVC alone, providing a physiological basis for distinguishing emphysema-predominant from airway-disease-predominant expressions of COPD. The present findings extend this concept beyond phenotyping. ESI was incorporated into MD-COPD not to identify an emphysema phenotype, but to determine whether information contained in the expiratory portion of the flow-volume curve could represent part of the structural dimension otherwise provided by CT. ESI need not reproduce an imaging measure to contribute useful information to a multidimensional classification; its value may derive from the physiological expression of structural abnormalities.

Another potentially useful property of ESI is its relative stability after bronchodilation. In the present cohort, ESI changed little after bronchodilator administration, consistent with previous observations in a smaller COPD population (24). This characteristic may be clinically relevant because bronchodilator responsiveness in COPD varies over time and may introduce uncertainty when used to define stable disease characteristics (25). Together with its independence from ethnic and anthropometric reference equations, the relative stability of ESI after bronchodilation supports its use as a physiological descriptor within a multidimensional classification.

Several features strengthen the interpretation of these findings. The large, well-characterized COPDGene cohort allowed MD-COPD, NoCT, and ESI classifications to be applied to the same 9,240 participants. Inclusion of both NoCT and ESI separated the consequences of removing imaging from those of replacing its contribution with physiological information. Thresholds were selected by agreement with MD-COPD without using clinical outcomes, and long-term follow-up allowed reclassification to be examined across mortality, exacerbations, and lung-function decline. Availability of visual and quantitative CT information also allowed the visual imaging criteria used by MD-COPD to be distinguished from quantitative structural abnormalities.

Several limitations should be considered. COPDGene enrolled current and former smokers aged 45–80 years with ≥10 pack-years, requiring validation in other populations. Although threshold selection used repeated cross-validation and was independent of clinical outcomes, the modified classification was developed and evaluated within the same cohort and requires external validation. MD-COPD served as the reference framework but is not a diagnostic gold standard; agreement therefore measures preservation of its classification structure rather than diagnostic accuracy, and discordance cannot establish that either approach is correct. ESI also showed substantially weaker discrimination of visual CT abnormalities with preserved spirometry, suggesting less complete physiological representation of the CT component when structural disease is less physiologically expressed. Respiratory deaths were few in some low-risk subgroups, limiting the precision of the corresponding estimates. Finally, these findings do not imply that ESI can replace chest CT when direct anatomical characterization is clinically required.

The ESI-based approach has potential practical implications because it requires neither additional physiological testing nor imaging, but extracts information from the same flow-volume curve obtained during conventional spirometry. When CT is unavailable or impractical, combining ESI with respiratory symptoms may therefore represent part of the structural dimension of MD-COPD without another diagnostic procedure. This may be particularly relevant in epidemiological studies, large cohorts, and settings where systematic CT assessment is not feasible. More broadly, conventional spirometry may contain clinically relevant information beyond FEV₁ and FEV₁/FVC that can characterize the physiological expression of structural lung disease.

In conclusion, replacing the CT component of MD-COPD with ESI preserved much of the original multidimensional classification and recovered clinically relevant information lost when imaging criteria were simply removed. Importantly, this was achieved not by introducing another diagnostic procedure, but by extracting additional information from the same spirometric examination used to assess airflow limitation. These findings support the concept that part of the structural dimension of COPD can be represented through its physiological expression and suggest a practical approach to multidimensional COPD assessment when chest CT is unavailable. External validation is required to establish the generalizability and clinical applicability of this CT-free framework.
