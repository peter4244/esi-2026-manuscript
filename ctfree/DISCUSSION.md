In this study, we sought to establish a multidimensional COPD classification that retains the advantages of the MD-COPD classification over the fixed ratio but that can also be deployed in the absence of chest CT data. We observed that this is achievable through use of the ESI, a descriptor derived from the morphology of the expiratory flow-volume curve that does not depend on ethnic or anthropometric reference equations. This paper has three main findings. First, the ESI-based reformulation preserves most of the advantages of the CT-based framework for mortality and exacerbations, generating a classification with comparable prognostic associations. Second, it is necessary to have ESI or some other effective surrogate for structural lung disease in a multidimensional classification. Otherwise, individuals with airflow obstruction and unrepresented emphysema or airway wall thickening will be misclassified and contaminate the AFL-only group, leading to a harmful underdiagnosis of individuals at high risk of COPD-related complications. Third, the benefit of ESI is primarily in improving the classification of individuals with spirometric obstruction, where ESI is a better surrogate for CT measures.

One of the advantages of the MD-COPD classification is in the reclassification of subjects with spirometric obstruction but no CT minor criteria into the no COPD category. The original MD-COPD classification demonstrated that this group did not have increased risk for overall or respiratory mortality, so proper reconstruction of the AFL-only group is one of the major areas where a CT-free classification succeeds or fails since the AFL-only group is defensible only while the participants in it are genuinely at low risk. This study shows that removing the CT criteria without an adequate surrogate, moves a substantial number of higher risk subjects into the AFL-only pathway, contaminating this low-risk group. This finding is consistent with prior observations visually assessed emphysema is associated with mortality in this cohort (19). The fat that the ESI classification successfully preserves AFL-only as a lower risk group means that multidimensional COPD classification with ESI in place of CT measures can still improve diagnostic specificity by distinguishing isolated airflow limitation from broader disease expression characterized by structural, spirometric, or symptomatic abnormalities.

The ESI-based reformulation demonstrated high agreement with the CT-based classification and maintained similar relationships with mortality and exacerbation risk, and its disagreements with that classification carry clinically relevant information rather than representing simple error. Within the preserved-spirometry subgroup, participants classified as COPD by both approaches experienced the highest observed event rates, whereas the discordant groups showed intermediate rates. The 612 participants that the ESI classification adds to the COPD-minor pathway do carry an adjusted all-cause hazard ratio of 1.59 (1.30 to 1.95) against participants both classifications call noCOPD, approaching the 1.94 (1.62 to 2.32) of participants both call COPD, while the 70 it misses are few and show no excess mortality. These discordant patterns should therefore not simply be viewed as false positive or false negative classifications, but as examples of the different biological information provided by structural imaging and ESI-based spirometric assessment.

CT and spirometry interrogate different aspects of the disease process. Chest CT directly visualizes structural abnormalities, including parenchymal destruction and airway changes (18), whereas ESI reflects the integrated physiological consequences of these abnormalities on expiratory flow generation. Loss of elastic recoil, small airway dysfunction, and heterogeneous regional abnormalities may all contribute to alterations in the shape of the expiratory flow-volume curve (22–24). The objective of this spirometric representation is therefore not to reproduce CT morphology, but to capture the functional consequences of structural abnormalities that determine clinical expression and prognosis. That relationship is not uniform across the disease spectrum. Associations between ESI and quantitative CT emphysema were weak among individuals with minimal structural abnormalities, and substantially stronger among participants with established airflow obstruction. The same pattern governs where an ESI criterion helps: ESI discriminated visual emphysema and airway wall thickening among participants with airflow limitation but not among those with preserved spirometry, where its area under the curve fell from 0.75 and 0.76 to 0.56 and 0.61, respectively. Imperfect correlation between CT and ESI shows that anatomical and spirometric measurements capture overlapping but distinct dimensions of COPD.

Another distinctive feature of ESI is its independence from acute bronchodilator response. In the present cohort, ESI was essentially unchanged after bronchodilation (mean ΔESI = −0.09), extending previous observations reported in a small COPD population (25). This stability suggests that spirometric curve morphology captures mechanical properties of the respiratory system that are not substantially influenced by reversible changes in airway caliber. This characteristic may be clinically relevant because bronchodilator responsiveness in COPD is variable over time and may introduce uncertainty when used to define stable disease characteristics (26). Together with its independence from reference equations, this makes ESI a better candidate for a structural criterion than a conventional spirometric index such as FEV₁ percent predicted, which would carry both dependencies into the classification.

These findings have important practical implications. Because there are many settings in which chest CT is not readily available, the ESI classification allows for multidimensional CT classification to be performed wherever spirometry and symptom assessment can be performed, including many clinical settings, large epidemiological studies, and clinical trials. Rather than replacing CT, this approach provides a practical strategy for extending multidimensional COPD assessment, including some resource-poor settings.

This study has several limitations. Although the ESI-based framework preserved the diagnostic and prognostic performance of the multidimensional classification, no single physiological descriptor can be expected to capture every aspect of CT-defined structural abnormalities. In particular, because ESI was originally developed to reflect the physiological consequences of emphysema, airway-predominant structural abnormalities with relatively limited emphysema may be less completely represented, and agreement is weakest for the COPD-minor pathway. The threshold values used to represent the CT-defined structural component were derived within the COPDGene cohort and require prospective validation in independent populations; the count threshold of two was selected by an objective model-fitting process, but it departs from the original framework. The present study was designed to reproduce the MD-COPD framework and therefore compared ESI with the visually assessed CT structural criteria used in that framework rather than with quantitative CT phenotyping approaches. Respiratory deaths are few in the low-risk groups, so several respiratory estimates rest on single-figure event counts. Accordingly, all observations based on fewer than 10 events are reported without confidence intervals and flagged as such.

In conclusion, a spirometric descriptor derived from expiratory flow-volume curve morphology preserved the contribution of the CT lung structural domain within a multidimensional COPD diagnostic framework while maintaining comparable prognostic associations. These findings support the concept that imaging-based and spirometry-derived descriptors can provide complementary representations of COPD and may enable broader implementation of multidimensional disease assessment.
