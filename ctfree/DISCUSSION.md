# Discussion — CT-free MD-COPD paper

*(new, in Pete's structure; language drawn from v15 where it fits)*

In this study, we sought to establish a multidimensional COPD classification
that retains the advantages of the MD-COPD classification over the fixed ratio
but that can also be deployed in the absence of chest CT data. We observed that
this is achievable through use of the Emphysema Severity Index, a descriptor
derived from the morphology of the expiratory flow-volume curve that is
calculated from absolute spirometric values and does not depend on ethnic or
anthropometric reference equations. This paper has three main findings. First,
that including ESI in a CT-free multidimensional classification avoids
contamination of the AFL-only group with higher risk COPD-major subjects whose
COPD diagnosis depends on CT structural criteria. Second, that the ESI-based
reformulation preserves the advantages of the CT-based framework for mortality
and exacerbations, generating a classification with comparable prognostic
associations. Third, that this is achieved almost entirely among participants
with airflow limitation, where ESI tracks the CT criteria it replaces and where
those criteria decide whether a COPD diagnosis is given.

*(new; closes with v15's AFL-only argument)*

The AFL-only group is where a CT-free classification succeeds or fails. It
exists to withhold a diagnosis from participants the fixed ratio would call
COPD, so it is defensible only while the participants in it are genuinely at low
risk. Removing the CT criteria without replacing them moves 833 participants
into it, and the group that receives them is no longer low risk: against a
reference common to all three classifications its adjusted respiratory hazard
ratio is 6.80 (95% CI: 3.44 to 13.46), where under MD-COPD and ESI-MD-COPD the
adjusted all-cause hazard ratios are 0.94 and 0.96. The raw counts say it more
plainly: 2 respiratory deaths under MD-COPD and 4 with ESI, against 34 without
CT. The participants who make the difference are those whose only minor
criterion was a CT finding, and visually assessed emphysema of this kind is
itself associated with mortality in this cohort (19). Retaining them supports
the concept that multidimensional COPD classification improves diagnostic
specificity by distinguishing isolated airflow limitation from broader disease
expression characterized by structural, spirometric, or symptomatic
abnormalities.

*(new; discordance framing from v15 ¶5-6)*

The ESI-based reformulation demonstrated high agreement with the CT-based
classification and maintained similar relationships with mortality and
exacerbation risk, and its disagreements with that classification carry
clinically relevant information rather than representing simple error. Within
the preserved-spirometry subgroup, participants classified as COPD by both
approaches experienced the highest observed event rates, whereas the discordant
groups showed intermediate rates. The 612 participants ESI-MD-COPD adds carry an
adjusted all-cause hazard ratio of 1.59 (1.30 to 1.95) against participants both
classifications call noCOPD, approaching the 1.94 (1.62 to 2.32) of participants
both call COPD, while the 70 it misses are few and show no excess mortality.
These discordant patterns should therefore not simply be viewed as false
positive or false negative classifications, but as examples of the different
biological information provided by structural imaging and spirometric
assessment. Discrimination alone would not have identified the better
classification: the symptoms-only classification had the highest C-index of the
four (Supplemental Table S4), which it earned by making COPD-major smaller and
more severe while moving 833 participants into a group named as not having COPD.
A C-index measures how well groups rank risk, not whether they mean what they
say.

*(v15 ¶2 and ¶4, near-verbatim, closing on where the benefit falls)*

CT and spirometry interrogate different aspects of the disease process. Chest CT
directly visualizes structural abnormalities, including parenchymal destruction
and airway changes (18), whereas ESI reflects the integrated physiological
consequences of these abnormalities on expiratory flow generation. Loss of
elastic recoil, small airway dysfunction, and heterogeneous regional
abnormalities may all contribute to alterations in the shape of the expiratory
flow-volume curve (22–24). The objective of this spirometric representation is
therefore not to reproduce CT morphology, but to capture the functional
consequences of structural abnormalities that determine clinical expression and
prognosis. That relationship is not uniform across the disease spectrum.
Associations between ESI and quantitative CT emphysema were weak among
individuals with minimal structural abnormalities, where limited biological
variability reduces the expected relationship between imaging and spirometric
measures, and substantially stronger among participants with established
airflow obstruction. The same pattern governs where an ESI criterion helps: ESI
discriminated visual emphysema and airway wall thickening among participants
with airflow limitation but not among those with preserved spirometry, where its
area under the curve fell to 0.56 and 0.61. Imperfect correlation between CT and
ESI should not necessarily be interpreted as disagreement, but as evidence that
anatomical and spirometric measurements capture overlapping but distinct
dimensions of COPD.

*(new; the objection a reader will raise)*

This raises the question of why FEV₁/FVC is not itself used as the replacement
criterion, given that it discriminates the CT findings at least as well as ESI
in almost every stratum (Supplemental Table S12). It cannot be, because it is
already the major criterion, and a second threshold on it fails on whichever
side of 0.70 it is placed. Above 0.70 it is met by every participant with
airflow limitation, so the AFL-only group empties entirely and the correction
the framework exists to make is destroyed. Below 0.70 it is met, by definition,
by no participant with preserved spirometry, so it cannot contribute to the
COPD-minor pathway at all. A replacement criterion has to carry information that
is not already spent on the major criterion, and has to reach participants on
both sides of it. A measure of flow-volume curve shape does; a second threshold
on the diagnostic variable does neither.

*(v15 ¶3, near-verbatim)*

Another distinctive feature of ESI is its independence from acute bronchodilator
response. In the present cohort, ESI was essentially unchanged after
bronchodilation (mean ΔESI = −0.09), extending previous observations reported in
a small COPD population (25). This stability suggests that spirometric curve
morphology captures mechanical properties of the respiratory system that are not
substantially influenced by reversible changes in airway caliber. This
characteristic may be clinically relevant because bronchodilator responsiveness
in COPD is variable over time and may introduce uncertainty when used to define
stable disease characteristics (26). Together with its independence from
reference equations, this makes ESI a better candidate for a structural
criterion than a conventional spirometric index such as FEV₁ percent predicted,
which would carry both dependencies into the classification.

*(v15 ¶9, near-verbatim)*

Beyond preserving the diagnostic and prognostic performance of the CT-based
framework, the present findings have important practical implications. Because
spirometry is inexpensive and more widely available than chest CT, ESI-based
multidimensional COPD assessment can be more readily applied in routine clinical
practice, primary care, epidemiological studies, and clinical trials.
Importantly, because ESI is derived from the same spirometric examination used
to assess airflow obstruction, the ESI-based framework requires only spirometry
and symptom assessment rather than the combination of spirometry, symptoms, and
CT imaging. Rather than replacing CT, this approach provides a practical
strategy for extending multidimensional COPD assessment to settings in which
structural imaging cannot be routinely incorporated.

*(v15 ¶10, updated to this analysis)*

This study has several limitations. Although the ESI-based framework preserved
the diagnostic and prognostic performance of the multidimensional
classification, no single physiological descriptor can be expected to capture
every aspect of CT-defined structural abnormalities. In particular, because ESI
was originally developed to reflect the physiological consequences of emphysema,
airway-predominant structural abnormalities with relatively limited emphysema
may be less completely represented, and agreement is weakest at the COPD-minor
boundary, where ESI is flat across the range of the visual emphysema scale at
which that criterion is set. The threshold values used to represent the
CT-defined structural component were derived within the COPDGene cohort and
require prospective validation in independent populations; the count threshold
of two was selected by the same fitting and departs from the three of the
original framework, and a different fitting objective would have selected a
different count. The present study was designed to reproduce the MD-COPD
framework and therefore compared ESI with the visually assessed CT structural
criteria used in that framework rather than with quantitative CT phenotyping
approaches. Respiratory deaths are few in the low-risk groups, so several
respiratory estimates rest on single-figure event counts and are reported
without intervals; the contrast that carries the argument is between 34 such
deaths and 2. Finally, all comparisons treat the CT-based classification as the
reference, which is appropriate for asking whether it can be reproduced without
CT but does not establish that it is correct.

*(v15 conclusion, near-verbatim)*

In conclusion, a spirometric descriptor derived from expiratory flow-volume
curve morphology preserved the contribution of the structural domain within a
multidimensional COPD diagnostic framework while maintaining comparable
prognostic associations, and did so without chest CT. These findings support the
concept that imaging-based and spirometry-derived descriptors can provide
complementary representations of COPD and may enable broader implementation of
multidimensional disease assessment.
