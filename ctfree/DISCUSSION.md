# Discussion — CT-free MD-COPD paper

*(Massimo's v4 (2) of 2026-10-03, his response to Surya Bhatt's critiques, with the fixes Pete approved; v5.)*

In this study, we examined whether the Emphysema Severity Index (ESI), derived from the expiratory portion of the spirometric flow-volume curve, could represent part of the information contributed by the CT criteria of the MD-COPD-CT framework when CT is unavailable. We observed that using ESI in place of CT criteria preserved many of the beneficial aspects of the MD-COPD-CT classification, especially among individuals with airflow limitation, but was less effective in reproducing the CT-based classification among individuals without airflow limitation.

The benefits of the MD-COPD-CT classification arise from the creation of the COPD-minor pathway, which confers the COPD label on individuals without airflow limitation but with high symptom burden or evidence of structural lung disease, and the AFL-only pathway, which removes the COPD diagnosis from relatively low-risk individuals with airflow limitation but no minor criteria. Our data show that incorporating ESI in place of the CT criteria in MD-COPD-CT is effective at reconstituting a “low-risk” AFL-only pathway, but less effective at isolating a “higher-risk” COPD-minor pathway. ESI reconstructed the MD-COPD-CT classification more closely than removing the CT criteria without replacement, particularly in the AFL-only pathway, demonstrating the utility of this approach in settings where CT is not available.

ESI describes the morphology of the expiratory portion of the flow-volume curve, which changes with loss of elastic recoil, small airway dysfunction, and differences in lung volume and thoracic gas compression (22–24). It discriminated the CT criteria better in individuals with airflow limitation than in those without airflow limitation (AUC 0.75 for both criteria, against 0.56 and 0.61). While ESI can serve as an index for the severity of emphysema (14, 15), the relationship between ESI, emphysema (measured both visually and via measures like %LAA-950HU), and airway wall thickness is complex. CT depicts structural abnormalities, whereas ESI describes their expression within expiratory physiology; the two should therefore not be viewed as interchangeable measurements. The association of ESI with airway wall thickening should not be interpreted as indicating that ESI directly measures airway disease.

Because ESI better discriminated the CT criteria in individuals with airflow limitation, its advantages over the MD-NoCT classification derive primarily from the characteristics of the AFL-only diagnostic pathway under MD-COPD-ESI. Without CT criteria available, the 833 MD-COPD-CT COPD-major participants whose only minor criteria were CT findings were all reclassified as AFL-only by MD-NoCT, producing an AFL-only group that was four times the size of MD-COPD-CT AFL-only (1,108 versus 275 participants). This transfer raised event rates in AFL-only and eliminated the primary diagnostic benefit of the AFL-only pathway, which is to remove the COPD diagnosis from individuals with airflow limitation who are at relatively low risk of COPD-related outcomes. By recovering part of the information contributed by the CT criteria, ESI reduced this discordant assignment and preserved the risk differential between the AFL-only and COPD-major diagnostic pathways.

The ability of ESI to reproduce the CT contribution was less complete in some diagnostic pathways. For the COPD-minor pathway, while MD-COPD-ESI did identify participants with higher risks of mortality and exacerbations relative to the noCOPD group, these unadjusted rates were lower than in MD-COPD-CT COPD-minor. For FEV₁ decline, faster decline than the reference was seen in MD-COPD-CT COPD-minor but not in MD-COPD-ESI or MD-NoCT COPD-minor. Even the MD-COPD-ESI AFL-only group, which otherwise performed quite well, had faster FEV₁ decline than the common reference, whereas MD-COPD-CT AFL-only did not.

ESI has attractive characteristics when CT is unavailable. It is calculated from the expiratory portion of the flow-volume curve recorded during standard spirometry, so it requires neither an additional test nor imaging, and because it uses absolute measurements it needs no ethnic or anthropometric reference equations. Unlike many other spirometric criteria, it is fairly insensitive to bronchodilation (25), which, in the context of COPD diagnostic tools, is a source of undesirable measurement variability (26). These properties are most relevant to epidemiological studies, large cohorts, and clinical settings where systematic CT assessment is not feasible.

This study has several strengths. All three classifications were applied to the same 9,240 COPDGene participants, and including the MD-NoCT classification distinguished the effect of removing the CT criteria from that of incorporating ESI in their place. Long-term follow-up allowed each reclassification to be examined against mortality, exacerbations, and FEV₁ decline. Visual and quantitative CT were both available, allowing the visual CT criteria used by MD-COPD-CT to be examined alongside quantitative emphysema.

Several limitations should be considered. COPDGene enrolled current and former smokers aged 45–80 years with ≥10 pack-years, and MD-COPD-ESI was developed and evaluated in the same cohort; although thresholds were cross-validated, external validation is required. MD-COPD-CT served as the reference but is not a diagnostic gold standard, so agreement measures how well its structure is preserved rather than diagnostic accuracy, and discordance cannot establish which approach is correct. Respiratory deaths were too few in AFL-only and COPD-minor for comparison between classifications. Finally, these findings do not imply that ESI can replace chest CT when anatomical characterization is clinically required.

In conclusion, replacing the CT criteria of MD-COPD-CT with ESI preserved much of the original multidimensional classification and recovered clinically relevant information lost when imaging criteria were simply removed. The recovery was concentrated in participants with airflow limitation, where ESI kept more than half of the participants whose COPD-major classification depended on CT criteria from being reassigned to AFL-only. Importantly, this was achieved by extracting additional information from the same spirometric examination used to assess airflow limitation, without introducing another diagnostic procedure. The findings suggest that the expiratory flow-volume curve contains clinically relevant physiological information about the structural dimension of COPD beyond conventional spirometric indices. External validation is required to establish the generalizability and clinical applicability of this framework.
