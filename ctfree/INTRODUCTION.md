Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a multidimensional disease in which airflow limitation, structural lung abnormalities, and respiratory symptoms provide complementary information on disease expression (1–6). Consequently, reliance on spirometry alone incompletely captures the phenotypic heterogeneity of COPD and has prompted the development of diagnostic approaches integrating multiple disease dimensions (7–10).

Recently, Bhatt and colleagues proposed a multidimensional diagnostic framework integrating spirometry, chest computed tomography (CT), and symptom burden (11). By combining airflow obstruction with respiratory symptoms and imaging evidence of emphysema or airway wall thickening, this framework identifies clinically relevant COPD beyond conventional spirometric criteria and improves prediction of mortality and lung function decline. The framework, which we refer to as the MD-COPD (multidimensional COPD) classification, was subsequently validated in an independent population (12). However, the requirement for chest CT may constrain implementation of this otherwise attractive diagnostic approach in routine clinical practice, large epidemiological studies, and healthcare settings where advanced imaging is unavailable or impractical.

The Emphysema Severity Index (ESI) is a continuous spirometric measure derived exclusively from the morphology of the expiratory flow-volume curve (13, 14). Unlike conventional spirometric indices, which quantify expiratory volumes or flows and require comparison with reference populations, ESI is calculated from absolute spirometric measurements and is intrinsically independent of ethnic and anthropometric reference equations. Previous studies have demonstrated close associations between ESI and quantitative CT measures of emphysema, together with independent prognostic value across different populations (15, 16). These observations suggest that ESI may provide a spirometry-derived representation of the structural information contributed by chest CT within a multidimensional diagnostic framework.

We hypothesized that ESI could be incorporated in a modified form of the MD-COPD classification for use where chest CT measurements are not available, retaining the prognostic advantages of MD-COPD over the fixed ratio classification. We tested this hypothesis in the COPDGene cohort by comparing four classifications of the same participants: fixed ratio, the previously published MD-COPD framework, a modified MD-COPD classification with the CT criteria removed without any substitute (NoCT classification), and another modified MD-COPD classification in which ESI is substituted for CT measures (ESI classification). We examined how well the NoCT and ESI classifications reproduce the four MD-COPD diagnostic pathways, and we quantified the raw and adjusted rates of all-cause mortality, respiratory mortality, and respiratory exacerbations within each classification. We observed that withholding CT information in the NoCT approach substantially degraded the prognostic performance of the MD-COPD classification, and use of ESI allowed for partial recovery of the CT-related information and restored most of the prognostic benefits provided by the MD-COPD classification.
