# DISCUSSION


In this study, we evaluated a reformulation of a recently proposed multidimensional COPD diagnostic framework by using a spirometric descriptor derived from the expiratory flow-volume curve in place of its CT-defined structural component. The main finding was that representing visually assessed CT structural abnormalities using the Emphysema Severity Index (ESI) preserved the overall diagnostic architecture of the original framework and generated a classification with highly comparable prognostic associations. The ESI-based reformulation demonstrated high agreement with the CT-based classification and maintained similar relationships with mortality, respiratory outcomes, and exacerbation risk. These findings suggest that imaging and spirometry may provide different but clinically meaningful representations of COPD expression.

CT and spirometry interrogate different aspects of the disease process. Chest CT directly visualizes structural abnormalities, including parenchymal destruction and airway changes (18), whereas ESI reflects the integrated physiological consequences of these abnormalities on expiratory flow generation. Loss of elastic recoil, small airway dysfunction, and heterogeneous regional abnormalities may all contribute to alterations in the shape of the expiratory flow-volume curve (21-23). Thus, imaging and spirometry represent different measurement domains that may capture complementary manifestations of the same underlying biological process. The objective of this spirometric representation is therefore not to reproduce CT morphology, but to capture the functional consequences of structural abnormalities that determine clinical outcomes and prognosis.

Another distinctive feature of ESI is its independence from acute bronchodilator response. In the present cohort, ESI was essentially unchanged after bronchodilation (mean ΔESI = −0.09 across n = 10,160 paired pre/post-bronchodilator measurements), extending previous observations reported in a small COPD population (25). This stability suggests that spirometric curve morphology captures mechanical properties of the respiratory system that are not substantially influenced by reversible changes in airway caliber. This characteristic may be clinically relevant because bronchodilator responsiveness in COPD is variable over time and may introduce uncertainty when used to define stable disease characteristics (26).

The relationship observed between ESI and quantitative CT measures of emphysema further supports the concept that structural imaging and spirometric measures provide related but non-identical information. Associations were weak among individuals with minimal structural abnormalities, where limited biological variability reduces the expected relationship between imaging and spirometric measures, but became progressively stronger among participants with established airflow obstruction and structural disease. Therefore, imperfect correlation between CT and ESI should not necessarily be interpreted as disagreement, but rather as evidence that anatomical and spirometric measurements capture overlapping but distinct dimensions of COPD.

The analysis of discordant classifications further illustrates this concept. Participants classified as COPD by the original CT-based framework frequently showed visually detected emphysema despite quantitative CT emphysema values within the normal range (27). This pattern reflects the ability of visual CT assessment to identify early or mild morphological abnormalities not captured by quantitative emphysema burden. Because the original framework assigns a structural criterion for even mild visually identified emphysema (11), quantitatively normal participants can still meet the CT-based structural component regardless of whether they are ultimately classified as Both-COPD or as Bhatt-only-COPD; the observation is a general property of the visual-CT criterion rather than a phenomenon unique to the discordant subgroup. Conversely, participants classified only by the ESI-based framework showed abnormalities in expiratory flow-volume curve morphology and a high symptom burden. These discordant patterns should therefore not simply be viewed as false-positive or false-negative classifications, but as examples of the different biological information provided by structural imaging and spirometric assessment.

Cross-classification analyses demonstrated that discordance between frameworks was associated with clinically relevant information. Participants classified as COPD by both approaches carried the greatest risk, whereas discordant groups displayed intermediate risk profiles. These findings suggest that agreement between imaging and spirometric representations identifies individuals with the strongest disease expression, while discordance may identify different manifestations within the COPD spectrum.

An additional observation supporting the multidimensional concept was the behavior of participants with airflow limitation alone. Participants classified as AFL-only-noCOPD did not demonstrate significantly increased risk of mortality or exacerbations under either the CT-based or ESI-based framework. This observation supports the concept that multidimensional COPD classification may improve diagnostic specificity by distinguishing isolated airflow limitation from broader disease expression characterized by structural, spirometric, or symptomatic abnormalities.

Secondary analyses evaluating ESI as a continuous measure provide additional insight into the potential role of expiratory flow-volume curve morphology in early disease assessment. Among smokers with preserved spirometry, higher baseline ESI was associated with accelerated subsequent decline in FEV₁ independent of baseline FEV₁/FVC. In contrast, continuous ESI added little beyond FEV₁/FVC for prospective exacerbation counts (joint-model IRR ≈ 1.01, p ≈ 0.70), indicating that the ESI signal that drives the categorical-framework exacerbation risk operates through the two-threshold rule rather than through per-unit continuous variation once airflow obstruction is accounted for. The FEV₁-decline finding suggests that ESI may provide an individualized spirometric signal of smoking-related lung injury before conventional spirometric thresholds are crossed. Such person-level information may be particularly relevant in smokers who are currently reassured by normal spirometric values despite evidence that clinically relevant smoking-related lung disease may already be present (28–31). Consistent with this framing, PRISm participants demonstrated greater longitudinal increases in ESI than GOLD 0 participants despite similar baseline values, suggesting that changes in flow-volume curve morphology may reflect evolving disease processes not captured by a single conventional spirometric classification. Together, these findings support the concept that physiological and structural markers may identify early smoking-related lung injury before conventional spirometric criteria for COPD are fulfilled.

Beyond preserving the diagnostic and prognostic performance of the CT-based framework, the present findings have important practical implications. Because spirometry is inexpensive, widely available, and routinely performed worldwide, ESI could facilitate multidimensional COPD assessment in routine clinical practice, primary care, epidemiological studies, longitudinal disease monitoring, and clinical trials, particularly where chest CT is unavailable, impractical, or unsuitable for repeated evaluation. Rather than replacing CT, this approach provides a practical strategy for extending multidimensional COPD assessment to settings in which structural imaging cannot be routinely incorporated.

This study has several limitations. Although the ESI-based framework preserved the diagnostic and prognostic performance of the multidimensional classification, no single physiological descriptor can be expected to capture every aspect of CT-defined structural abnormalities. The threshold values used to represent the CT-defined structural component were derived within the COPDGene cohort and require prospective validation in independent populations. The present study was designed to reproduce the multidimensional framework proposed by Bhatt and colleagues and therefore compared ESI with the visually assessed CT structural criteria used in that framework rather than with quantitative CT phenotyping approaches. The ESI-only-COPD (n = 95) and AFL-only-noCOPD (n = 170) subgroups are small; the associated confidence intervals are correspondingly wide and do not exclude modest clinically meaningful risks, so quantitative comparisons involving these subgroups should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, although the secondary analyses suggest potential applications of ESI for identifying smokers at increased risk of disease progression, these findings require prospective confirmation.
